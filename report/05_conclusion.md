# Conclusion and Limitations

## Conclusion

Our central finding is that the best way to spend a limited vaccination budget depends on the shape of the contact network. When a population contains a few highly connected people, as in our scale-free network, vaccinating the 10% most connected people reduced the average final size from 0.84 to 0.22 (74%), compared with only 19% for vaccinating 10% at random, and it also lowered the peak number infected from about 128 to about 16 to 20 people out of 300 and made major outbreaks much less likely. When contacts are organised in tight groups with a few shortcuts, as in our small-world network, vaccinating the people who bridge groups (highest betweenness) worked best (0.44 against 0.59 for random vaccination), although with 30 repetitions this difference is suggestive rather than conclusive. When everyone has a similar number of contacts, as in our random network, targeting gave no advantage over random vaccination, which is what we would expect because no person is structurally special. Targeting also loses most of its advantage when transmission is very strong (beta = 0.20), because the disease reaches almost everyone anyway.

In practical terms, this suggests that knowing who is highly connected is valuable when contacts are very uneven, and not worth much when they are even. Our second, independently written model (see the comparison section) agrees that targeted vaccination beats random vaccination on scale-free networks by a large margin.

## Limitations

The first group of limitations concerns the model. Vaccination is perfect and instant: vaccinated people are fully immune before the outbreak begins, whereas real vaccines are partial and take time to roll out. The network is fixed during an outbreak, so people never change their behaviour, for example by staying at home when ill or avoiding hubs. Everyone is treated the same apart from their position in the network, and recovery follows a simple per-step probability, which gives a wide spread of infection lengths. We also tested only one vaccination coverage (10%), one recovery rate (gamma = 0.1) and populations of 300 people with about six contacts each, so we cannot say how the conclusions change at other coverages or for larger populations. The targeting strategies assume that the whole network is known in advance, which is rarely true for real contact patterns.

The second group concerns the evidence. Each combination was run 30 times, and because an outbreak either dies out quickly or takes off, the confidence intervals are wide. We therefore only treat differences larger than about 0.1 as reliable. The calibration is also limited: our model matched the size of the peak and approximately its timing for the 1978 boarding-school outbreak, but not the final size, because on a network with enough contacts to give such a fast outbreak, an outbreak that takes off reaches almost everyone (0.97), whereas only 67% of the boys were confined to bed. Possible explanations are that some boys were already immune, that not every infection led to bed rest, and that a boarding school has structure (dormitories, classes) that our simple networks do not capture. We did not test these explanations. The comparison model also differs from ours on the random network, where it finds that targeted vaccination clearly beats random vaccination; this could be due to the different settings (100 people and 20% vaccinated against 300 and 10%) or to the different implementations, and we have not tested which.

## Future work

The most useful extensions would be partial vaccine effectiveness and delayed rollout, networks that change during the outbreak, contact networks built from real data (for example a school or workplace), several coverage levels so that we can find how many people need to be vaccinated for targeting to matter, and a calibration that also allows part of the population to start immune.
